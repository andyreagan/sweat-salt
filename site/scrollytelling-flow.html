Sweat out, water in

What happens to the salt in your blood when you sweat, drink, and take salt. Scroll.

The figure is your body water. Its colour is how much sodium is dissolved in it: blue is dilute, red is concentrated, and grey-green is the normal range for blood, 135 to 145 mmol/L. Drops leaving are sweat, coloured by how salty it is. Bottles are what you drink. Every frame comes from a standard mass-balance equation; the sources are at the bottom.

This is you, as water.

About 60% of your weight is water, and dissolved in it is sodium at about 140 mmol/L. Your body holds that number in a narrow band. Much lower and you get sick and confused; much higher and you get thirsty and hot.

Sweat is far more dilute than blood. Average sweat is about 45; very salty sweat, the kind that leaves white crust on dark kit, is about 90. A sports drink is about 20, an electrolyte mix about 65. The scale at the bottom shows each.

Sweat for three hours. Drink nothing.

Sweat is mostly water with a little salt, and it is always less salty than blood. So every drop that leaves takes away more water than sodium, and the blood left behind gets saltier. The number goes up. Three hours of this with nothing to drink and you are above the normal range.

The body's answer to this is thirst.

Drink half of it back.

Plain water coming in dilutes what's left. Four bottles over the three hours, emptying one after another, make the rise slower. They don't stop it. The blood still ends up saltier than it started.

Drink 70%. The colour holds.

There is a drinking rate at which the dilution from the bottle exactly matches the concentration from sweating, and the blood stays where it started. For average sweat it's about 70% of what you sweat. Call it the crossover.

Drink less than that and your blood gets saltier no matter what else you do. Drink more and it gets more dilute.

Drink 90%. Now it turns blue.

Past the crossover, plain water dilutes the blood and the number goes down. This is the only situation in which salt in the drink changes blood chemistry. Most people never reach it, because drinking 90% of what you sweat is hard to do.

Sweep the drinking rate.

Same three hours, same sweat, drinking from nothing up to everything. Red on the left, blue on the right, grey-green at 70%.

Body size doesn't move that point. Nor does how long you go, or how much you sweat. Only how salty the sweat is.

A rugby player.

Big body, heavy sweat, and a game that allows one bottle in 80 minutes. He puts back 15% of what he loses. His blood gets saltier, up to the top of the normal range, with no salt taken at all.

Now he takes salt to "replace what he lost".

All of it, in the one bottle he's allowed. That makes a drink close to seawater. His blood goes up further, past the top of the normal range.

Salt made this worse. His problem was never salt. It was three litres of water he couldn't drink.

A salty marathoner.

Some people's sweat is nearly twice as salty as average. Their crossover is lower, under 50%. Running a hot marathon and drinking a litre an hour, about 70% of their sweat, plain water dilutes them. Their blood goes down, and drops out of the bottom of the normal range before four hours.

A common rule of thumb says salt only matters in events longer than four hours. For this runner, the rule is wrong.

About 1.2 grams an hour holds them flat.

That's nearly three times the sodium of a sports drink, in every bottle. The bottles are visibly saltier, and the blood holds steady in the normal range the whole way. This is the one case in the story where salt is doing real work.

A heavy sweater.

Some people lose two and a half litres an hour in the heat. Drinking a litre an hour puts back less than half of it. The level drops fast and the blood gets saltier, past the top of the normal range. Well under the crossover, so thirst is the signal, not salt.

Replace all that sodium, and it goes higher still.

Salt cannot put water back. What helps here is pace, cooling, a gut trained to take more, and drinking before the start.

Same drinking, different sweat.

Three hours, 70% replaced, plain water, and sweat getting saltier from left to right. People with low-sodium sweat end up saltier than they started. People with salty sweat end up more dilute.

The crossover is a property of your sweat, and that is the one thing a sweat test can tell you.
