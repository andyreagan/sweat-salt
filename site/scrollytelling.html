Why your blood gets saltier when you sweat

A scroll through the physiology behind Do I need salt?, with the numbers moving.

Everything below comes from one mass-balance equation, the one McCubbin's 2025 review of sodium for athletes is built on. Scroll.

Sweat is salty. Blood is saltier.

Plasma sits at 140 mmol/L of sodium. Average sweat is about 45. Even a very salty sweater, the kind with white crust on their kit, is at 90.

A sports drink is about 20.

So sweating concentrates your blood.

Three hours at 1.4 L/h with no drinking, average sweat. You lose 4.2 L of water but proportionally less sodium, and plasma sodium climbs to about 152.

Nothing about salt intake changes the direction. You are removing water faster than sodium.

Drink half of it back. Still rising.

Replace 50% of the sweat with plain water and the climb is gentler, but it is still a climb.

Drink 70%. Flat.

There is a replacement fraction where the water you drink exactly balances the water you lose relative to sodium. For average sweat it is about 70%. That is the crossover.

Drink 90%. Now it falls.

Past the crossover, plain water dilutes you. This is the only situation in which salt in the bottle matters for blood chemistry, and it is the situation most people never reach.

The crossover depends on one thing: how salty you sweat.

Not body mass, not duration, not sweat rate. Rearrange the equation and they all cancel.

Average sweat crosses at 70%. Salty sweat at 54%. Very salty at 41%.

The rugby player.

McCubbin's own example: 95 kg, sweating 2.5 L/h for 80 minutes, allowed 500 mL of water. That is 15% replacement, far under any crossover.

Plasma sodium rises to 144 with no salt. Replace all the sodium he sweats and it rises to 148, above the reference range. Salt makes this case worse.

The salty marathoner.

70 kg, sweat sodium 80 mmol/L, running a hot marathon at 1.4 L/h and drinking 1.0 L/h. That is 71% replacement, above their 47% crossover.

Plasma sodium falls through 135 at about 3.5 hours and finishes at 134.6.

The decision tree never asks.

The review's Figure 3 starts with one question: is the event over 4 hours? If not, "season to taste."

This runner is under 4 hours the whole way. The tree's later branches would have caught them, but they never get there.

What 1.2 g/h does.

Holding plasma sodium flat for this runner takes about 1,200 mg/h, which is 120 mg per 100 mL of what they drink, nearly three times a sports drink and at the edge of what people will keep drinking. It is the one case in this story where the salt is doing real work.

The heavy sweater.

87 kg in a heat chamber at 2.4 L/h, drinking 1 L/h. That is 41% replacement, under the crossover for average sweat.

Plasma sodium rises to 146 with no salt. Replace 100% of sweat sodium and it reaches 154. This athlete's problem is water, and no amount of salt fixes water.

Where everyone lands.

Plot each athlete by how salty they sweat and how much they replace. Above the curve, salt matters. Below it, season to taste.

Most people, most days, are below.
