Decision path

    Assumes no urine output, no metabolic water, potassium unreplaced, and sodium taken evenly through the event. Modelling only. Few trials have measured plasma sodium at high sweat rates with salty drinks, and none at the extremes this page will let you enter. Not medical advice.

    Where the numbers come from

    The model. Plasma sodium is computed from mass balance of water, sodium and potassium (Kurtz and Nguyen [1]), as applied to exercise in McCubbin's review [2]. Baker, Lang and Kenney [3] validated it, matching predicted to measured serum sodium within 0.2 mmol/L in runners held at 0 to −4% body mass on drinks up to 30 mmol/L sodium. Outside that range, especially with very salty drinks or large deficits, treat the lines as bounds.

    Sodium does raise plasma sodium. With near-full fluid replacement, a 60 mmol/L drink raised plasma sodium by 0.8 mmol/L; a 21 mmol/L drink let it fall by 1.5 [4]. Given a 100 mmol/L drink with full fluid replacement, the kidney held osmolality by retaining water and expanding extracellular fluid; it did not excrete the extra sodium [5]. Excreting sodium needs water, which the hypohydrated athlete cannot spare; exercise itself suppresses urine output. Hypernatraemia above 145 mmol/L was found in 35% of desert stage-race runners after one 42 km stage, three times the rate of hyponatraemia [6]. Osmotically inactive sodium stores exist, but the evidence is that they respond to volume loss rather than to sodium concentration, and on a timescale of days [2].

    The deficit target is yours to set. Blinded to their hydration status and infused to hold it steady, cyclists at 0, −2 and −3% body mass rode a 25 km time trial at 33 °C with no difference in performance [7]. The older evidence for a hard 2% limit largely confounds thirst with hydration [8]. Weight-bearing sport and severe heat leave less room, but the number is a judgement call.
